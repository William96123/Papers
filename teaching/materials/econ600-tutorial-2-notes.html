Tutorial 2 Notes (2026–27)

William Duan

September 15, 2026

These notes document what was covered in the tutorial session, for the benefit of future TAs. They are not intended as study material for students.

Topics

The continuity axiom. Definition: \(\succsim\) is continuous if for every \(x\) the upper contour set \(U(x) = \{\hat{x} : \hat{x} \succsim x\}\) and the lower contour set \(L(x) = \{\hat{x} : x \succsim \hat{x}\}\) are both closed. (Under completeness this is equivalent to the strict contour sets being open, since only then is the complement of \(U(x)\) the strict lower contour set; without completeness the two formulations come apart.) How to prove a preference is not continuous, using lexicographic preference on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) as the worked example. Interpretation and significance of the axiom. Careful reading of the theorem that \(\succsim\) is rational and continuous if and only if it can be represented by a continuous utility function, ending on a concrete example of a discontinuous utility function representing a genuinely discontinuous preference.

Objective

Introduce the continuity axiom and give students hands-on practice working with contour sets and constructing counterexample sequences. Train careful reading of representation theorems — in particular, that “can be represented by a continuous utility function” is an existence statement, not a statement about every representation.

Comments

Outline of what was done, for reference:

  • Interpretation of continuity. Small changes in bundles do not reverse the preference ordering; preferences are preserved under limits. This framing motivates why the axiom is stated in terms of closed contour sets.

  • Proving discontinuity: lexicographic preference. On \(\mathbb{R}^2\), \(x \succsim y\) iff \(x_1 > y_1\), or \(x_1 = y_1\) and \(x_2 \geq y_2\). Fix \(x\) and take \(y^n = (x_1 + \tfrac{1}{n},\, x_2 - c)\) for some \(c > 0\). Each \(y^n \succ x\), so \(y^n \in U(x)\) for all \(n\), but \(y^n \to (x_1, x_2 - c) \prec x\), so the limit escapes \(U(x)\): the upper contour set is not closed. Pedagogical point emphasized in class: to find a suitable sequence, draw the (upper) contour set first and look for a boundary point that does not belong to it.

  • Reading the iff carefully. One direction is easy: if a continuous \(u\) represents \(\succsim\), then \(U(x) = u^{-1}([u(x), \infty))\) and \(L(x) = u^{-1}((-\infty, u(x)])\) are preimages of closed sets under a continuous function, hence closed. But the theorem does not say that a continuous preference only has continuous representations. Counterexample: on \(\mathbb{R}\), \(u(x) = x\) and \(\tilde{u}(x) = x + 4 \cdot \mathbf{1}\{x > a\}\) represent the same (continuous, monotone) preference, yet \(\tilde{u}\) is discontinuous — utility is ordinal, and only the order matters.

  • A discontinuous preference. Closing example: the preference on \(\mathbb{R}\) represented by \(u(x) = x\) for \(x \leq 1\) and \(u(x) = x - 1\) for \(x > 1\). For \(x \in (0,1)\) the lower contour set is \(L(x) = (-\infty, x] \cup (1, x+1]\), which is not closed, so the preference itself is discontinuous — even though the corresponding upper contour sets are closed. A useful side effect of this example: it shows that closedness of the upper contour sets does not imply closedness of the lower ones (nor vice versa) — the two halves of the continuity definition are logically independent, which is why the definition requires both. Practical warning drawn from this: when the utility function is discontinuous, check both families of contour sets before concluding anything about the preference.

  • Proof strategy for the hard direction. For rational \(+\) continuous \(\Rightarrow\) existence of a continuous representation, we discussed why proof by contradiction does not work and why a direct, constructive proof is needed (construct a candidate \(u\), verify it represents \(\succsim\), verify it is continuous). The full construction was only previewed, not replicated. Last year this proof was the centerpiece of Tutorial 2.

The two examples in the third and fourth bullets were chosen deliberately as a pair: students routinely misread the representation theorem as “continuous preference \(\Leftrightarrow\) continuous utility function” at the level of individual functions. Seeing a continuous preference with a discontinuous representation, and then a discontinuous preference where discontinuity shows up in only one family of contour sets, is an effective way to head this off.

Tutorial 4 Notes (2026–27)

William Duan

September 29, 2026

These notes document what was covered in the tutorial session, for the benefit of future TAs. They are not intended as study material for students.

Topics

A methods session rather than a new-material session: how to prove a statement about an optimization problem directly from the structure of the problem, instead of solving the problem in closed form and verifying the statement afterwards. Three worked questions, chosen so that the same lesson appears in three different disguises. (i) A problem-set question on a one-person production economy, covering existence of a solution, convexity of the solution set, and uniqueness, none of which require a demand function. (ii) The slide result that under quasilinear utility the demand for every non-numeraire good is independent of wealth. (iii) The slide result that when the utility function is homogeneous of degree \(1\), the value function of the UMP is homogeneous of degree \(1\) in wealth. Time permitting, a problem-set question comparing a commodity tax with a wealth tax, where the problem set itself stages the contrast between a closed-form comparison and a revealed-preference argument.

The other two results are on the lecture slides. These notes record which points are worth making and why, and leave the derivations to whoever is building the session.

Objective

One methodological point, made three or four times. Students arrive with the undergraduate protocol: posit a functional form, solve the first-order conditions, obtain demand in closed form, then inspect it to check whatever was asked. The protocol is not so much wrong as inapplicable — most of the statements in this course are quantified over a class of utility functions, so a closed-form calculation establishes one instance and not the claim; many of the problems have no closed form at all; and even when a closed form exists, producing it usually buries the one hypothesis the question was asking about. The objective is to replace that protocol with a different one: read the statement carefully enough to see what is quantified over what, identify the structural feature of the problem that the conclusion is actually about, and argue from that feature directly.

Comments

It is worth writing the undergraduate protocol on the board explicitly at the start — assume a form, solve, verify — and naming the three ways it fails, so that the examples can be pointed back at it rather than each being taught as a separate trick. Students do not experience themselves as following a protocol; they experience themselves as doing the only thing there is to do. Naming it as one option among others is most of the work. The replacement offered was: write down what is to be proved, separating hypothesis from conclusion and noting what is universally quantified; find the structural feature the conclusion is really about; prove it from that feature and stop. Across the four questions below that feature is different each time, which is the point of doing four.

Two diagnostics were given to students, both of which they can run without help, and both worth repeating whenever the occasion arises. First: if your proof mentions a functional form and the statement does not, you have proved a special case. This is the most common failure mode and it is self-detectable. Second: if you finish and find you never used one of the stated hypotheses, work out which of the two things happened — you made an error, or the hypothesis is not needed. Either is worth knowing, and the habit is what makes the minimal-assumptions questions tractable.

The four questions, with the points worth drawing out of each:

  • The production-economy question. Placed first because the direct route is not merely better but forced: no functional form is given, so there is nothing to differentiate and no first-order condition to write. Students cannot fall back on the old protocol, which makes it safe ground for demonstrating the new one before applying it where the old one would have worked. Read the question’s own preamble aloud — it asks for the minimum assumptions and says outright that functional forms, derivatives and Lagrangians are not wanted — since a fair number of students will have skipped it.

    The point to draw out of the existence part is that the choice of formulation is part of the answer. A formulation that writes consumption as equalities rather than inequalities has already assumed that nothing is thrown away, which is local nonsatiation smuggled in through the notation. The lesson is that the formulation determines the hypothesis list, so it has to be justified rather than assumed.

    For the convexity part, the useful move is to ask what assumption on the primitives makes the feasible set convex. That ties the problem back to Tutorial 3, so the assumptions stop looking arbitrary. For uniqueness, the principle worth stating is that uniqueness comes from making exactly one of the weak inequalities in the convexity argument strict, and there is usually more than one place to do it.

  • Quasilinear utility, demand independent of wealth (slides). Here the lesson bites, because a closed-form route exists and most students will have taken it. The direct argument is three lines — use monotonicity in the numeraire to eliminate it from the problem, and observe that wealth survives only as an additive constant, so the argmax cannot depend on it. It needs no differentiability, no concavity, no interiority and no functional form.

    The point most worth making is about the consumption set. The statement puts the numeraire on all of \((-\infty,\infty)\), and students read this as a typographical curiosity; it is in fact the hypothesis that makes the theorem true, because with a non-negativity constraint on the numeraire the reduced problem inherits a wealth-dependent constraint and the conclusion genuinely fails at low wealth. Asking the class why the consumption set is written that way is the fastest available test of whether anyone has read the statement.

    It is worth putting the first-order-condition route on the board next to the direct one. It is a correct proof, and comparing them side by side makes the cost visible: it buys differentiability, interiority and enough concavity for sufficiency, in exchange for nothing. Also flag the pairing with the problem-set question that runs the implication in the opposite direction — from observed wealth-independent demand back to the class of utility functions — since students tend to treat the two as unrelated questions.

  • Homogeneous utility, value function homogeneous of degree 1 in wealth (slides). The proof is a change of variables and nothing else: the budget set scales with wealth, the objective is homogeneous, and the result falls out. Exactly two facts do the work, and it is worth pausing to name them, because everything else — the number of goods, the shape of the utility function, differentiability, convexity — is irrelevant. A quick comprehension check that worked well: ask which of the two facts fails if a quantity constraint on one good is added, and whether the result survives.

    The closed-form route here is Cobb-Douglas: compute the indirect utility function, observe it is linear in wealth, declare victory. Worth saying explicitly that this establishes one case out of infinitely many, and worse, leaves the impression that the result has something to do with Cobb-Douglas when it has nothing to do with any functional form. The formulation I used: a verified example is evidence that a statement is worth trying to prove, and is never a proof.

    The subtlety worth spending real time on is that this is a statement about a representation, not about a preference. The value function is not an ordinal object — replacing the utility function by its square gives the same preference and a value function homogeneous of degree \(2\) — so the degree-\(1\) property belongs to the utility function chosen. What belongs to the preference is homotheticity, i.e. homogeneity of degree \(1\) of demand, since the argmax survives strictly increasing transformations while the max does not. This is a direct callback to the Tutorial 2 discussion of representation theorems and should be flagged as such. Combined with the fact that a preference is homothetic if and only if some representation is homogeneous of degree \(1\), the correct statement is that for a homothetic preference one may always choose a representation whose value function is linear in wealth. Worth writing on the board in that form, because the loose version (“homothetic preferences have value functions linear in wealth”) is false as stated, and is what students will otherwise carry away.

  • Commodity tax versus wealth tax. Kept as the closing item and treated as expendable if the session runs long. It suits the purpose because the problem set stages the contrast itself, asking for the welfare comparison first under a specific functional form and then for arbitrary locally nonsatiated preferences by revealed preference. Students who work in order will have done both halves without noticing that the general argument contains the specific one. Making them go back and say what the functional-form computation was for is the exercise, and it costs nothing to set up because they have already done both halves. Reading off from a proof exactly how much it can and cannot deliver, here whether the comparison comes out weak or strict, is a skill in its own right and this is a good place to practise it.

    Two things to say in closing. The shape of the argument is not new — a bundle was available and not chosen, therefore the chosen bundle is at least as good — which is the revealed-preference logic from Tutorial 1 reused; saying so helps, because students tend to file WARP as week-one abstraction rather than as a tool they are expected to reach for. And the economics behind the comparison, the excess burden of commodity taxation, returns later in the course.

Practical notes

The ordering was chosen deliberately and is the part of this session most worth preserving. The production-economy question goes first because the old protocol is unavailable there, so the new one can be demonstrated without any argument about whether it is necessary. The two slide results go second, because there the old protocol would have worked, and the comparison is the whole lesson. The tax question goes last, because by then the point has been made three times and the students can supply the short argument themselves.

Both problem-set questions were live when this session ran. They were discussed as method rather than delivered as solutions — the structure of each argument was worked out on the board and the write-up left to the students. A future TA should check the posting and due dates before deciding how much of the problem set to put up; if the timing is unfavourable, the two slide results plus one substitute question of the same shape will carry the session.

If the session runs short, the most useful filler is to take any statement from the slides that students have already seen proved by computation and ask for a direct proof instead. The supply is large — homogeneity of degree \(0\) of Marshallian demand, Walras’ Law, monotonicity of the expenditure function — and the exercise is the same every time.

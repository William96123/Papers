Tutorial 6 Notes (2026–27)

William Duan

October 13, 2026

These notes document what was covered in the tutorial session, for the benefit of future TAs. They are not intended as study material for students.

Topics

The whole session went to the optional coding exercise in Part II of Problem Set 3: testing whether each subject in an experimental data set makes choices consistent with GARP. Five segments. (i) Reading the data — each subject faces the same 22 two-good budget lines, recorded by their intercepts, and picks one bundle on each. (ii) Extracting revealed-preference comparisons from a single budget line, and three ways of storing them: a list per observation, a single coded matrix, and a pair of matrices for weak and strict comparisons. (iii) What a violation looks like, namely a cycle of weak comparisons containing at least one strict link, and what to do about chosen bundles that sit slightly off their budget line. (iv) A cycle-finding algorithm: depth-first search from a starting observation, merging cycles that turn out to be purely weak, and removing a node once no violation passes through it. (v) Two ways to measure how bad a violation is, cycle length and the money pump index.

The student-facing write-up of all of this is the GARP coding guide, which was revised from the 2021 handout to follow this session’s sketch. It contains the algorithm step by step and the pitfalls, so nothing below reproduces it. These notes record only the teaching side.

Objective

The session’s theme is turning a definition into a procedure. Students have by this point seen GARP stated formally on the slides and used it in pen-and-paper questions with three or four observations, where a violation is found by staring at a diagram. With 22 observations per subject and 82 subjects, staring no longer works, and the exercise forces them to say precisely what they were doing when they stared. The thing to draw out is that GARP is a statement about cycles in a directed graph, so testing it is a graph search. Once that sentence lands, the rest of the session is bookkeeping.

It is worth connecting this explicitly to Tutorial 5. Afriat’s theorem says that GARP is the entire testable content of utility maximization on finite data: if the test passes, a well-behaved utility function exists, and there is nothing further to check. So the code students write is not one test among many; it is the whole empirical content of the theory they have spent five weeks on. That framing makes an optional exercise feel less optional.

Comments

  • Why spend a session on code. The exercise is optional and language-agnostic, and first-year programming backgrounds are uneven. I did not teach syntax. The session stayed entirely at the level of the algorithm, on the view that a student who can state the procedure precisely can get any language (or a lightweight AI model, which the guide explicitly allows) to implement it, while a student who cannot state it will produce code that passes on the clean subjects and fails silently on the rest. This is a judgement worth keeping: the session is about precision of thought, and code is just the medium that punishes imprecision.

  • The single-budget example. I began with one budget line and four bundles: one chosen on the line, one strictly inside, one elsewhere on the line, and one outside. The first three give a strict comparison, a weak comparison and nothing respectively. The point I pressed hardest was the fourth: the chosen bundle being affordable and the outside bundle not affordable tells us nothing, and in particular it does not tell us that the outside bundle is weakly preferred to the chosen one. That needs its own check on the outside bundle’s budget. Treating “unaffordable” as “revealed worse” or “revealed better” is the natural slip here, and it is far easier to catch on the board than in someone’s code.

  • Representations. I offered three ways of storing the comparisons and recommended the third, two 0/1 matrices (one for weak and one for strict). The single matrix with codes 0, 1, 2 holds the same information, but every later step then has to decode it, and the two-matrix version makes the next step natural: the weak matrix is the adjacency matrix of the directed graph that the search runs on, and the strict matrix is the lookup table used to check a cycle once found. It is worth saying out loud that every strict link is also a weak one, so the strict matrix is contained in the weak one; students who build them independently sometimes get this wrong.

  • Bundles off the budget line. Recorded choices are rounded, so they rarely lie exactly on their budget line. In the session I offered two fixes: set the diagonal of the weak matrix to one by hand, or allow an epsilon of slack. When writing up the guide afterwards I found a cleaner statement that should replace both in future sessions: compare each bundle’s cost against what was actually spent at the chosen bundle, not against the full budget. This makes the diagonal correct automatically, and it removes a class of spurious violations — for example, a subject who chooses the identical bundle on two budgets, a hair inside both, shows up as strictly preferring each to the other under the full-budget comparison. A future TA should present the spending comparison directly rather than the two fixes I gave.

  • The search. The algorithm as presented: start a depth-first search from some observation along the weak links; whenever it returns to the start, check the cycle’s links against the strict matrix; if any is strict, stop with a violation; if all are weak, the observations on the cycle are revealed indifferent, so merge them into one node and continue; once the search from a node finds nothing, remove the node. I drew the search itself as a tree — go to the bottom of one branch, back up one step, try the next — which was enough for students who had not seen DFS before.

    Two details deserve explicit emphasis. First, a link into or out of a merged node stands for several original links and is strict if any of them is; the board reminder was to recheck each one. Second, a strict link between two members of a merged node is already a violation, since they lie on a common weak cycle. The second was not on the board and was added in the guide. Both are the kind of thing a student’s code gets wrong without anyone noticing.

    A future TA should know that the textbook approach is different and arguably simpler: compute the transitive closure of the weak matrix (Warshall’s algorithm, as in Varian’s 1982 implementation), and the data violate GARP exactly when some observation is revealed weakly preferred to another that is directly strictly revealed preferred to it. With 22 observations the cost is trivial. The search-and-merge version has two advantages: it hands back the violating cycle itself as evidence, which the closure does not do without extra work, and the merge step makes students say why weak cycles are harmless. Either is defensible. The closure version is less error-prone for weak programmers, and is worth mentioning as an alternative even if the search is what gets taught.

  • Measuring violations. The session closed with two ways to grade a failure rather than just record it. Cycle length, where a two-step cycle is a direct contradiction and a long chain a subtler one, and the money pump index: shrink each budget on the cycle until the next bundle stops being strictly affordable, and the total shrinkage relative to total spending is the money an arbitrageur could extract per lap. The motivation that worked was that a pass/fail test treats a rounding slip and a wild inconsistency the same, and the data contain both. The guide adds Afriat’s efficiency index (shrinking every budget at once), which was not covered in the session; a future TA with time could add it, since it is the measure students will meet in the empirical literature.

Practical notes

This session works only if students have the data and the problem set in front of them; I put the first rows of the data on screen and worked from them. A future TA should run their own implementation on the data before the session, since students will ask why their counts differ.

If time is short, cut the extensions segment; the guide covers it adequately in writing. The single-budget example and the off-budget pitfall should not be cut, since those are where student code actually goes wrong.

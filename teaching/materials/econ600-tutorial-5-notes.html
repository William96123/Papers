Tutorial 5 Notes (2026–27)

William Duan

October 6, 2026

These notes document what was covered in the tutorial session, for the benefit of future TAs. They are not intended as study material for students.

Topics

Four segments, running from welfare measurement to the recoverability of preferences from choice data. (i) A high-level discussion of why consumer surplus is not a well-defined welfare measure once more than one price moves, and why the compensated measures do not suffer from the problem. (ii) The duality chain assembled as a single diagram — Marshallian demand, indirect utility, the expenditure function and Hicksian demand, with Roy’s identity, the envelope theorem and Shephard’s lemma as the arrows between them — derived from the identity relating the value and expenditure functions. (iii) Recovering the indirect utility function from Marshallian demand by differentiating along one of its level sets, which yields an ordinary differential equation whose only input is observed demand. (iv) Afriat’s theorem, approached through the geometric construction of a rationalizing utility function, ending at the point where the constructed function is seen to take a minimum-of-affine-functions form.

This session was free-standing: it was not tied to a problem set, and no time was given to Problem Set 2 even though it was due three days later. The technical side of the welfare material is on the lecture slides, which also state GARP formally.

Objective

The session has an obvious seam in the middle — welfare on one side, Afriat on the other — and it is worth telling students what holds the two halves together, because they will not see it otherwise. The common question is how much of a consumer’s preference can be recovered from what we observe her do, and the segments answer it at progressively weaker levels of data. The duality chain says that the four standard objects are interconvertible, so observing any one of them is observing all of them. The differential equation in segment (iii) says that the Marshallian demand function alone pins down the shape of the indifference map, though not the utility numbers attached to it. Afriat’s theorem says that even a finite set of observed choices suffices, provided it satisfies GARP. Welfare measurement is then the application that motivates the whole apparatus: the measure we want is defined on an object we cannot observe, and duality is what gets us there from one we can.

Comments

  • Welfare, kept deliberately high-level. The slides already handle the technical content of this material well — the definitions of equivalent and compensating variation, the integral expressions, the comparisons — and I did not think I had much to add on that side. The session instead supplied an interpretive frame: what the measures are for, and why one of them misbehaves. This is a judgement call worth recording as such, because the general question of where a tutorial adds value relative to the lectures recurs every week, and re-deriving what the slides already derive is the default way to waste a session.

    The frame used: consumer surplus is an area under a demand curve, so computing it when several prices move means integrating along a path through price space, and there is no reason the answer should be independent of the path chosen. Marshallian demand carries both the substitution and the income effect, and the income effect of a change in one price feeds into the response to the next, so the order in which you integrate changes the number you get. Compensated demand carries only the substitution effect and the problem does not arise. I used an improvised linear three-good demand system with two prices moving to make the point concrete.

    An aside that landed well, and is worth keeping: suppose the price of coke and the price of rent both rise by two percent. Take the coke increase first, and its income effect lowers the consumer’s rent consumption, so the rent integral is evaluated along a lower demand curve than it would have been. Take them in the other order and the same logic runs the other way. The order of integration is not a technicality; it is the income effect leaking between goods. Two concrete goods with wildly different budget shares make this much easier to see than an abstract \(p_1\) and \(p_2\).

    What was not done, and should be flagged for anyone building from these notes: only equivalent variation was defined, compensating variation was not, and the reason the compensated line integral is path-independent — symmetry of the Slutsky matrix, or equivalently the expenditure function serving as a potential — was asserted rather than explained. Either would be a reasonable addition if the session has room.

  • The duality chain. The centrepiece, and the thing most worth preserving from this session: the four objects drawn in one boxed diagram, with the named results labelling the arrows between them rather than being presented as four separate theorems. Students meet Roy’s identity, the envelope theorem and Shephard’s lemma in different lectures and file them as unrelated machinery; seeing them as the edges of a single closed loop is the point of putting them together.

    The derivation I used runs through the identity saying that utility evaluated at the cheapest bundle attaining a given utility level returns that level. Differentiating it totally in prices, recognising the derivative of the expenditure function as Hicksian demand, and rearranging produces Roy’s identity directly. This is worth doing rather than quoting, because it makes visible that the loop closes — the same identity, read in two directions, gives both of the results it connects.

    Two annotations to make explicitly while drawing, since both are invertibility claims that students skate over. The value function is strictly increasing in wealth, so for a fixed price vector exactly one wealth level attains a given utility, which is what licenses passing from the value function to the expenditure function. The expenditure function is strictly increasing in the utility level, for the same obvious reason, which licenses the return trip. Without those two observations the diagram is a picture of four objects rather than an argument.

  • Recovering the indirect utility function. A short segment and a satisfying one. Fix a level set of the value function — the set of price-wealth pairs delivering some fixed utility — and differentiate along it. The resulting differential equation has a right-hand side that involves only Marshallian demand, so demand data are sufficient to trace out the level sets.

    The conclusion to state carefully, because the careless version is wrong: what the equation recovers is the shape of the indifference map, not the utility numbers attached to it. That is exactly what one should expect given everything the course has said about ordinality, and it is worth saying so out loud, since it turns a technical result into a confirmation of something they already believe. It also sets up Afriat, which is the same claim with the data thinned from a whole demand function down to finitely many observations.

  • Afriat’s theorem. Stated in the usual four-part equivalent form. The thing to emphasise is which implication carries the content: the step from GARP, an empirically checkable condition on the data, to the existence of a utility function that is not merely locally nonsatiated but continuous, strictly increasing and concave. Getting such a well-behaved rationalization out of a finite consistency check is genuinely surprising and students should be told that it is.

    The approach that worked was to build the construction up geometrically, in order, letting each picture fail before fixing it. Start with a single observed bundle on a single budget line and ask what it would mean to rationalize it: draw an indifference curve tangent to the budget at the observed point. Add a second observation on a different budget line and do the same. Now notice that the two smooth curves drawn this way cross, which no indifference map may do. The repair is to replace the smooth curves with piecewise-linear ones, taking the slope from the budget line through the observed bundle — that part is forced, since the tangency is what rationalization means — and leaving the intercept free. The free intercept is the multiplier, and what it does is locate the kink. Once the kinks are positioned so that the curves do not cross, the whole family can be drawn. Finally, show a configuration violating the axiom and let the class try: there is no way to position the kinks, and the impossibility of drawing the family is the geometric content of the theorem.

    That last picture is the one that does the teaching, and it is worth leaving time for students to attempt it themselves before showing them it cannot be done.

    The session ended at the observation that the utility function assembled this way is a minimum over the observations of affine functions, one per observation, with the multiplier as the coefficient. Why a minimum rather than a maximum is worth a moment: the lower envelope of a family of affine functions is concave and the upper envelope is convex, and concavity is precisely the property the strong form of the theorem promises. A quick sketch of each envelope makes the point faster than any argument.

    Nothing beyond that was covered — the verification that the constructed function actually rationalizes the data, and that it has the three properties claimed, was not attempted. In past years those verifications have been set as a problem set question rather than done in tutorial, so a future TA should check whether the current year’s Problem Set 3 does the same before deciding to spend session time on them.

  • GARP against WARP. Flagged in passing that GARP is the stronger axiom, but not demonstrated, and GARP itself was not restated since the lecture slides give it formally. Worth being candid about why the demonstration was skipped: a configuration satisfying WARP while violating GARP appears to require at least three goods, which makes it hard to put on a board. The obstacle looks to be a real theorem rather than a failure of ingenuity — with two goods and budget exhaustion the axioms essentially coincide (Rose, 1958), and the standard separating example (Gale, 1960) uses three goods — but a future TA who wants to present this should check those references before relying on them, and should be aware that the finite-data versions of these statements need more care than the demand-function versions.

    If the point is worth making properly, the options are to present a three-good numerical example as a table of choices rather than a diagram, or to leave the axiom comparison to the problem set and simply assert the ranking here, which is what was done.

Practical notes

The session was free-standing, built around neither a problem set nor a specific lecture, and it works as a consolidation session: everything in it had already appeared on the slides in some form, and the value added was in assembling the pieces into one diagram and one question. If a future year’s schedule is tighter, segments (i) and (iii) are the compressible ones; the duality diagram and the Afriat construction are what justify the session.

Problem Set 2 was due three days after this session and received no time at all. That was not a considered decision so much as a consequence of the material running long, and a future TA should decide deliberately rather than by default — a short logistics slot at the start costs little.

On ordering: welfare first works because it motivates wanting the compensated objects, which motivates the duality chain, which is then the natural place to draw the diagram. Afriat sits at the end because it is the most self-contained segment and the easiest to truncate, which is what happened. Anyone planning this session should plan to stop at the minimum-of-affine-functions form and treat anything past it as a bonus.

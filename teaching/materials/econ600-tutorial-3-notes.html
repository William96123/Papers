Tutorial 3 Notes (2026–27)

William Duan

September 22, 2026

These notes document what was covered in the tutorial session, for the benefit of future TAs. They are not intended as study material for students.

Topics

Basic properties of the utility maximization problem (UMP). Existence of a solution from continuity of \(u\) and compactness of the budget set. Walras’ Law: the budget constraint binds at any solution, and why monotonicity rather than local nonsatiation is the hypothesis that delivers it. Definitions of convex and strictly convex preferences; convexity implies the solution set is convex, strict convexity implies the solution is unique. The relationship between convex upper contour sets and quasiconcavity of the utility function, including where the “quasi” in quasiconcave comes from. A brief, explicitly non-examinable discussion of upper hemicontinuity of the demand correspondence. Finally, two recurring errors from the first problem set.

Objective

Assemble the standard toolkit for the UMP and, in each case, make explicit which hypothesis is doing the work. Connect the preference-level assumptions (convexity) to their utility-level counterparts (quasiconcavity), and reinforce that the latter is the ordinal notion. Address two recurring errors from the first problem set while it is still fresh.

Comments

Outline of the UMP material, for reference:

  • Existence. If \(u\) is continuous and the budget set \(B(p,w) = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^L_+ : p \cdot x \leq w\}\) is compact, Weierstrass gives a solution. The point worth belabouring is where compactness comes from: \(B(p,w)\) is closed by construction, and bounded only because \(p \gg 0\). With some \(p_\ell = 0\) the budget set is unbounded and (under local nonsatiation) the problem can fail to have a solution. Students tend to treat existence as automatic, so it is worth naming \(p \gg 0\) as a genuine hypothesis rather than a notational convention.

  • Walras’ Law. At any solution \(x^*\) the budget constraint binds: \(p \cdot x^* = w\). In this course local nonsatiation is stated globally on \(\mathbb{R}^L\) — for every \(x\) and every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(y \in \mathbb{R}^L\) with \(\|y - x\| < \varepsilon\) and \(y \succ x\) — with the non-negativity constraint imposed separately, as part of the consumption set rather than as part of LNS. Stated this way, LNS alone is not sufficient for Walras’ Law, and monotonicity is what delivers it.

    The reason is worth going through slowly, because it is a good lesson in reading the logic of a proof rather than its conclusion. The contradiction argument supposes \(p \cdot x^* < w\) and looks for a nearby improvement that is still affordable. LNS does supply a nearby improvement, but only somewhere in \(\mathbb{R}^L\): at a point on a face of the orthant that improving bundle may have a negative coordinate, in which case it is not a feasible alternative at all and no contradiction follows. So the argument establishes only that the solution cannot sit strictly inside the budget set. It does not say which part of the boundary the solution has to lie on, and in particular it does not rule out a face of the orthant. Monotonicity closes the gap by producing an improving bundle inside \(\mathbb{R}^L_+\) directly.

    The discriminating example is \(u(x) = -x_1 - x_2\), which is locally nonsatiated on \(\mathbb{R}^2\) but whose solution is \(x^* = (0,0)\), leaving the budget entirely unspent. Existence is not the issue here — the optimization is still over the compact set \(B(p,w)\).

    One distinction worth drawing explicitly while doing this: having budget slack and lying in the topological interior of the budget set are not the same condition. They coincide away from the axes, but come apart exactly on the faces of the orthant, which is where the whole question lives.

    The least costly repair worth mentioning is weak monotonicity — more is never worse — which is milder than full monotonicity and still does the job. If \(x^*\) has budget slack, adding a little of every good gives a bundle that is weakly better, hence also a solution, and that bundle sits in the strict interior of the budget set; LNS then applies there, since a whole ball around an interior point is feasible, and the rest of the argument goes through unchanged. There may be weaker fixes, but this is probably the weakest one that is easily interpretable.

  • Convexity. Definitions given: \(\succsim\) is convex if \(x \succsim y\) implies \(\alpha x + (1-\alpha) y \succsim y\) for all \(\alpha \in [0,1]\); strictly convex if for \(x \neq y\) with \(x \succsim y\), \(\alpha x + (1-\alpha) y \succ y\) for all \(\alpha \in (0,1)\). Convexity gives a convex solution set: if \(x\) and \(y\) both solve, any convex combination is affordable (the budget set is convex) and at least as good, hence also a solution. Strict convexity gives uniqueness: if \(x \neq y\) both solved, the midpoint would be affordable and strictly better than \(y\), contradicting optimality of \(y\).

  • Convex upper contour sets and quasiconcavity. \(\succsim\) is convex exactly when every upper contour set \(U(x)\) is convex, which is exactly quasiconcavity of any \(u\) representing \(\succsim\): \(u(\alpha x + (1-\alpha) y) \geq \min\{u(x), u(y)\}\). On the “quasi”, the explanation used in class was graphical rather than algebraic. A concave function is a smooth hump: it rises to a peak and curves downward everywhere. A quasiconcave function keeps the outline — it is still essentially single-peaked, with the highest point in the middle and no interior dip — but gives up the curvature, so it may be cone-shaped rather than hump-shaped, with straight, kinked or flat stretches. “Quasi” marks exactly that: concave in silhouette, not in the inequality. A concrete way to produce the picture is to take a concave dome and apply a strictly increasing convex transformation; the level sets are unchanged, so the function is still quasiconcave, but the surface gets pulled up into a cone. That construction is also the algebraic point in disguise, worth having in reserve for students who ask: concavity is a statement about the magnitudes of utility and is therefore cardinal, while quasiconcavity depends only on the level sets and is therefore ordinal — which is why convexity of preferences corresponds to quasiconcavity rather than concavity. Concavity implies quasiconcavity; the converse fails.

  • Upper hemicontinuity (flagged as non-examinable). Closed with a short discussion of the demand correspondence \(x(p,w)\) being upper hemicontinuous. Framing used: UHC is the natural generalization of continuity to set-valued maps. For a compact-valued, locally bounded correspondence it amounts to the closed-graph property — if \((p^n, w^n) \to (p,w)\) and \(x^n \in x(p^n, w^n)\) with \(x^n \to x\), then \(x \in x(p,w)\) — which is visibly the same shape as “the limit of the values is a value of the limit,” and collapses to ordinary continuity when the correspondence is single-valued. Emphasized that the result becomes much more useful precisely when the UMP has a unique solution: the correspondence is then a function and UHC is continuity of demand. Students were told explicitly they are unlikely to be tested on this; it was included to keep the single-valued and set-valued pictures from looking like unrelated machinery.

Two recurring errors from the first problem set

  1. Strict preference under the Pareto ordering. Given \(x \succsim y \iff x_i \geq y_i\) for all \(i\), students derived \(x \succ y \iff x_i > y_i\) for all \(i\), having read the negation of a universal as a universal. The correct statement is \(x_i \geq y_i\) for all \(i\) and \(x_j > y_j\) for at least one \(j\). The tell to give students: if your \(\succ\) does not rank \((2,1)\) strictly above \((1,1)\), you have derived strong Pareto dominance instead. Worth doing carefully — the same definition returns with Pareto efficiency and the welfare theorems.

  2. A cycle is not a violation of transitivity. On a question about aggregating two individuals’ preferences, students exhibited a cycle \(x \succsim^* y \succsim^* z \succsim^* x\) and declared transitivity violated. A cycle in a weak relation is not a failure of transitivity — it is what indifference looks like. To refute transitivity one must display a triple where the conclusion fails, i.e. \(a \succsim^* b\), \(b \succsim^* c\), and \(\neg(a \succsim^* c)\); that requires both agents to strictly prefer \(z\) to \(x\). The general lesson was stated out loud: to refute a universally quantified implication you must exhibit the instance where the conclusion fails, not merely produce a configuration that feels paradoxical.
